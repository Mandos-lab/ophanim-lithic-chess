Quick rules

Sixteen pieces each on an 8×8 board: fifteen men and one Dux. A piece moves any distance along a row or column, never jumping. Trap an enemy piece between two of yours in a straight line and it is captured. Capture the enemy Dux, or leave your opponent with one piece or no move, and you win. These are reconstructed rules, and the Dux rule is a house rule: see “About these rules” below.

Game log

    History of Ludus Latrunculorum

    Ludus latrunculorum, the “game of the little soldiers”, was a Roman two-player board game of capture. Latrunculus is a diminutive of latro, “mercenary” or “brigand”. It is believed to be a variant of the earlier Greek games petteia and pessoi. That is a belief, not a proven line of descent.

    The sources are poems and passing remarks, not a rulebook. Ovid, in the Ars Amatoria (3.358), has “a single counter is lost when caught between two enemies”. The Latin praise-poem Laus Pisonis describes the game at about lines 190–208, with glass counters. Varro, in De Lingua Latina X, compares the grid of the board with the table used to teach noun declensions. Plato (Republic 487b) uses the Greek game as a picture of a debater who is finally cornered and cannot move.

    Boards have been found in the ground: sixteen at the fort of Vindolanda in Northumberland, among them a 7×9 board found in 2019, and one at Stanway in Essex. No piece set or rule tablet has come with them.

    How to play

    The board

    An 8×8 grid. Cyan fills the two rows nearest you, amber the two rows at the far side: fifteen men and one Dux each, sixteen pieces. The Dux stands on the back row, the tall double-pyramid.

    Moving

    Cyan moves first, then you alternate. Move one piece any distance in a straight line along its row or column, through empty squares only. Pieces never jump and never move diagonally. The Dux moves the same way.

    Capturing

    When your move leaves an enemy piece directly between the piece you moved and another of your pieces, in a straight line, that enemy piece is removed. One move can capture several pieces. Your Dux can help to flank.

    Safe between enemies

    You may move a piece into the gap between two enemy pieces. Only the player who moves can capture, so it is safe there.

    Winning

    Capture the enemy Dux and you win at once. You also win if your opponent is down to one piece, or has no legal move (the old Latin phrase is ad incitas redactus, brought to a standstill). After 160 moves the side with more pieces wins, and equal pieces is a draw.

    Strategy

    Guard the Dux

    Losing it loses the game, so keep a friend beside it in both directions a flanker could use. A Dux in the corner of a row of men is hard to enclose.

    Look for the second piece

    Every capture needs two of your pieces on one line with the enemy between. Before you move, check what your opponent can reach in a single slide.

    Use the open lines

    Rows and columns are your roads. A piece on an open line can swing across the board in one move and close a trap from a distance.

    Hunt the Dux with two

    The enemy Dux falls to the same flank as any man. Bring two pieces onto one line around it, and make your opponent spend moves to defend.

    Computed

    Every sum above can be checked by adding the letters. An 8×8 board has 64 cells, and 64 = 4³ = Αλήθεια. 430 is also the sum of Αριθμός (number) and Μαθηματικά (mathematics).

    Model

    The framework reads the 64 cells and the 64 of Αλήθεια together, as a board where a capture follows from position alone. That reading fits only the 8×8 reconstruction. Other reconstructions use other boards, so the link is not general.

    Not claimed

    That any ancient player or author made these links, or that the Greek or Roman games were designed around them.

    About these rules

    Nobody knows exactly how Ludus Latrunculorum was played. Capture by flanking an enemy piece between two of yours is the best-supported point. The rest is a modern reconstruction, and the scholars disagree: Bell and Eger-Harsch suggest 17 pieces a side on an 8×7 board, Kowalski an 8×12 board with 12 men and a Dux, Schädler an 8×8 board with 16 to 24 pieces, and the Museum Quintana in Künzing sixteen pieces each. This page takes an 8×8 board (Schädler) and sixteen pieces each (Quintana), made of fifteen men and a Dux (Bell, Kowalski). Rook-like movement is the usual reconstruction. The rule that capturing the Dux ends the game, and the 160-move limit, are my own choices and are not attested. Treat it as one honest guess at the game, not as the Roman rulebook.

    The reconstruction summary follows Wikipedia’s article “Ludus latrunculorum” and should be checked against the original publications (Falkener 1892; Bell 1960; Kowalski; Schädler) before it is cited. The two-page PDF with the sources is in the Library, under Mandos’ stuff.
