Quick rules

Roll four pyramid dice: the number of marked tips pointing up is your move, 0 to 4 (a zero loses the turn). Bring all seven pieces along your path and off the far end, by exact count. Landing on an enemy piece in the middle lane sends it back to the start. The central rosette is safe, and any rosette gives another roll.

Game log

    History of the Royal Game of Ur

    The Royal Game of Ur takes its name from the ancient Sumerian city of Ur, in what is now southern Iraq. Sir Leonard Woolley found five boards in the Royal Cemetery there in the 1920s. The finest, inlaid with shell, red limestone and lapis lazuli, is in the British Museum, and these boards date from about 2600–2400 BCE. That puts the game among the oldest board games with a surviving board, close in age to Senet.

    The same twenty-square board turns up far beyond Ur: in Iran, Egypt (where it is carved on the back of several Senet boxes, including some from Tutankhamun’s tomb), Crete, and in later versions as far as India. A form of it was still played in Kerala within living memory. People played it for roughly three thousand years.

    The rules came from a cuneiform clay tablet in the British Museum, written in Babylon in 177 BCE by the scribe Itti-Marduk-balāṭu. Irving Finkel, a curator there, translated it in the early 1980s and reconstructed the game (published in 2007). The tablet is some two thousand years younger than the boards. It copies an earlier description by another scribe, Iddin-Bēl, and it ties certain squares to omens, such as good fortune. Finkel’s version combines the tablet’s rules with the equipment found in the Ur graves: seven pieces a side and pyramid dice. Some details remain uncertain. This page uses a Finkel-style version.

    The game outlived Mesopotamia. A form of it called Asha was played by the Jewish community of Kochi (Cochin) in southern India until the 1950s, when many of them left for Israel, and it is the last known living form of the game. It kept twenty squares but gave each player twelve pieces, and the squares were laid out a little differently. The exact Asha layout is not documented, so the “Asha-style” option here keeps the Ur board and rules and only changes the piece count to twelve.

    How to play

    The board

    Twenty squares: a block of 3×4 and a block of 2×3 joined by a narrow bridge. Each player has fourteen squares to walk along: four private squares, eight in the shared middle lane, and two private squares at the end.

    Pieces and dice

    Seven pieces each, all starting off the board. Four pyramid dice, each with two of its four tips marked. Count the marked tips pointing up. The chances are 0: 6.25%, 1: 25%, 2: 37.5%, 3: 25%, 4: 6.25%.

    Moving

    Move one piece forward by the number rolled, or enter a new piece from off the board. You may not land on your own piece. With no legal move, or a roll of zero, your turn passes.

    Capturing

    In the middle lane, landing on an enemy piece sends it back off the board, to start again. Pieces on the private squares are never at risk, because only you can enter them.

    Rosettes

    There are five rosettes. Landing on one gives you another roll. The central rosette (square 8) is also a safe square: a piece there cannot be captured, and an enemy cannot land on it.

    Bearing off

    A piece leaves the board only by an exact count from the last square. The first player to bring all seven pieces home wins.

    Strategy

    Hold the central rosette

    A piece on the middle rosette is untouchable and blocks the lane for your opponent. Many players try to take it early and keep it.

    Mind the gap behind you

    A piece in the middle lane is in danger from enemy pieces one to four squares behind it, most of all two behind (37.5%). A move that puts a piece out of reach is often worth more than a longer one.

    Chase rosettes

    Landing on a rosette gives a free roll, and some squares are reachable only by a 4, so a 2 or 3 into a rosette is a gift.

    Keep pieces in reserve

    Pieces waiting off the board are completely safe, and you can enter them whenever the roll fits. Do not rush all seven into the shared lane at once.

    The ΦΥ ΨΩΉ reading

    Everything in this section is interpretation: numbers, geometry and Greek letter-values read onto the board as part of the ΦΥ ΨΩΉ framework. It is not Assyriology, and the people of Ur did not use these correspondences. Enjoy it as a way of thinking about the board, not as history.

    A moon city

    Ur’s patron god was the moon god Nanna (called Sin in Akkadian). Mesopotamian months followed the moon and ran 29 or 30 days. The reading sets the game beside other counting games with a 30: Senet has 30 squares, and Tavli has 30 checkers.

    Three zones

    The 20 squares are a 4×3 block (12), a 2×3 block (6) and a two-square bridge. Each player owns six private squares (four at the start, two at the end), and the eight squares of the middle lane are shared.

    Five rosettes

    Five squares carry an eight-petalled rosette. Landing on one gives another roll, and the central one is safe. Greek rhodon (rose) comes to 294.

    Chance and skill

    Four pyramid dice supply the chance, and the choice of move supplies the skill. Greek tychē, chance or fortune, comes to 1308.

    About these rules

    This version follows Irving Finkel’s reconstruction of the rules from the Babylonian tablet, with simple choices of my own: the five rosettes, the safe central rosette, the exact count to bear off, and a zero roll passing the turn.
