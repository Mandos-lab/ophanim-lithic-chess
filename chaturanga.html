    About this game

    Chaturanga means “four limbs” in Sanskrit, the four arms of an army: foot soldiers, horses, elephants and chariots. It is usually dated to about the 6th century CE in northern India, and it is the best-supported ancestor of chess. It went to Persia as Shatranj, then through the Islamic world into Europe, where it became modern chess by about the 15th century.

    No rulebook from that time survives. The rules here are a reconstruction from later sources, and scholars differ on details. This page uses the common baseline: no check rule (you simply win by taking the Raja), a pawn that reaches the far side becomes a Mantri, and a player left with only a Raja loses. If you have no legal move the game is a draw. That last point is our assumption.

    Because the Mantri and Gaja can only step one or two squares, the game is slower and more positional than modern chess. Watch your Raja: nothing warns you before it is taken, except the red mark this page adds.
