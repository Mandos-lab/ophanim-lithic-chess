Quick rules

Sixteen pieces each on an 8×8 board. A piece moves any distance along a row or column, never jumping. Trap an enemy piece between two of yours in a straight line and it is captured. Leave your opponent with one piece, or with no move, and you win. These are reconstructed rules: see “About these rules” below.

Game log

    History of Petteia and Polis

    Petteia (also called Polis, the Greek word for a city) was one of the great board games of ancient Greece. Its name turns up in Greek writing from about 450 BCE: the earliest known mention is in a comedy by Cratinus, staged around 443 or 442 BCE. Plato, Polybius and others praised it as a game of real tactical skill.

    Plato uses it as a picture in the Republic. Socrates compares being out-argued to losing at petteia: the weaker player is slowly hemmed in until he has no move left. The game’s pieces were called “dogs” (κύνες), and the later writer Julius Pollux, in his Onomasticon, says the board was called the polis and was laid out as a grid, probably with a great many dogs on each side. Painted Greek pottery shows heroes such as Achilles and Ajax playing at a board, but nothing written explains those pictures, so scholars disagree about which game they show.

    No complete set of rules survives, and no board is certain to have been found. What the sources suggest is a game for two with no dice, in which pieces were captured by being enclosed from two sides, and in which keeping your pieces together mattered more than racing ahead. The Romans played a close relative, ludus latrunculorum. Modern players have filled the gaps with careful guesses, and this page uses one of them.

    How to play

    The board

    An 8×8 grid of sixty-four squares. Cyan fills the two rows nearest you, amber the two rows at the far side, sixteen pieces each.

    Moving

    Cyan moves first, then you alternate. Move one piece any distance in a straight line along its row or column, through empty squares only. Pieces never jump and never move diagonally.

    Capturing

    When your move leaves an enemy piece directly between the piece you moved and another of your pieces, in a straight line, that enemy piece is removed. One move can capture several pieces at once, in different directions.

    Safe between enemies

    You may move a piece into the gap between two enemy pieces. Only the player who moves can capture, so it is safe there.

    Winning

    You win when your opponent is down to a single piece, because one piece can no longer enclose anything, or when your opponent has no legal move. After 160 moves, the side with more pieces wins, and equal pieces is a draw.

    Strategy

    Keep your pieces together

    A piece with a friend beside it, in the line an attacker needs, cannot be enclosed. Lone pieces are the ones that get captured.

    Look for the second piece

    Every capture needs two of your pieces on one line with the enemy between. Before you move, check what your opponent can reach in a single slide.

    Use the open lines

    Rows and columns are your roads. A piece on an open line can swing across the board in one move and close a trap from a distance.

    Do not run ahead

    A piece that reaches the far side alone is often cut off. Advance in pairs, and keep a few pieces back as guards.

    The ΦΥ ΨΩΉ reading

    Everything in this section is interpretation: numbers, geometry and Greek letter-values read onto the board as part of the ΦΥ ΨΩΉ framework. It is not classical scholarship, and the pieces of evidence for the game itself are in the history above.

    A city of sixty-four

    The board is called the polis, the city, and an 8×8 grid has 64 squares, which is 4³ and the same count as the chess board. Greek polis comes to 390.

    Dogs on guard

    The pieces are the city’s dogs, and a dog is safest beside another dog. Greek kynes, dogs, comes to 675.

    Capture by two

    No single piece captures. It takes two pieces on a line, with the enemy between them. The reading sets this beside the pairing idea in the framework: a pair is the smallest unit that can act.

    About these rules

    Nobody knows exactly how Petteia was played. This page keeps what the sources suggest: a grid board, two colours of “dogs”, no dice, and capture by enclosing a piece. The rest is a modern reconstruction with simple choices of my own: an 8×8 board, sixteen pieces each (Pollux may mean more), rook-like movement, enclosure along rows and columns only, and the 160-move limit. Treat it as one honest guess at the game, not as the Greek rulebook.
