I · A king and twice as many enemies

The Asymmetric Game

Chess gives both armies the same men and the same aim. Tafl does not. A small force of defenders guards a king in the middle of the board, and an army twice their size surrounds them. The defenders win if the king breaks out to safety; the attackers win if they take him. Every piece, the king included, moves like a rook, and pieces are captured by being sandwiched in a straight line.

The name tells the story. Old Norse tafl means a table or a board game, and is borrowed from Latin tabula. The king piece is the hnefi, a word that also means “fist”. Together they make hnefatafl, “the king’s table”. For about eight hundred years, from roughly the 4th to the 12th century CE, tafl games were played across Scandinavia, Iceland, Britain, Ireland and Lapland, until chess arrived and pushed them aside.

Tafl probably grew out of the Roman game ludus latrunculorum, the “game of soldiers”, which also captured by enclosure. That link is a widely held guess, not a proven line of descent.

III · The game in the sagas

Skald, Riddle and Golden Pieces

Orkneyinga saga

Verse by Rögnvald Kali Kolsson, 12th century

Earl Rögnvald, who ruled Orkney, boasts in a verse of nine skills. Tafl comes first (Tafl emk orr at efla, roughly “I am eager to play tafl”), and runes, books, smithing, skiing, shooting, rowing, harp-playing and poetry follow. A noble was expected to play well.

Hervarar saga

The riddle of Gestumblindi

Odin, disguised as Gestumblindi, tests King Heiðrek with riddles. One asks: who are the maidens that fight weaponless around their lord, the dark ones always defending him and the fair ones attacking? The answer is the pieces of hnefatafl.

Völuspá

Poetic Edda

The seeress sees the gods at the dawn of time playing tafl in the meadow of Iðavöllr, rich in gold. After Ragnarök, when the world is born again, the surviving gods find their golden pieces waiting in the grass.

The meadow of Iðavöllr

A Game Older Than the World’s End

In the poem the same game opens the first age and the second. Tafl frames the whole life of the cosmos: played, lost, and played again.

IV · What the ground has kept

Boards, Bone and Glass

Gokstad

Norway · about 900 CE

A board from the Gokstad ship burial carries a tafl grid on one side and a Nine Men’s Morris board on the other. Games were buried with the powerful.

Ballinderry

Ireland · 10th century

A small yew-wood board of 7×7 squares with a peg hole in each, decorated with interlace carving. Pegged pieces would stay put on a moving ship or a lap.

Birka

Sweden · Viking Age

Graves at the trading town of Birka hold gaming pieces of bone, antler and glass, set among a warrior’s weapons: strategy counted as a martial skill.

Lindisfarne

England · 8th to 9th century

A piece of swirled blue and white glass, topped with white droplets, found in an excavation announced in 2020. Lindisfarne suffered the first great Viking raid in 793, but the piece is not proven to be linked to it; it may have been dropped by a Norse raider, or owned by a local who had taken up Norse customs.

V · Keep scrolling

How the Game Is Won

  1. The siegeAttackers ring the board. The king and his guard sit in the middle. The attackers move first.
  2. MovingEvery piece slides like a rook: any distance along a row or column over empty squares, never diagonally, never jumping.
  3. The sandwichA piece is captured when an enemy move leaves it between two enemy pieces in a line. The attacker slides in from the left and the defender is taken.
  4. The escapeThe king wins by reaching a corner refuge (marked with Tiwaz, the rune of victory). On the 9×9 board any edge square will do.
  5. The siege closesThe attackers win by capturing the king. On the 11×11 board that takes all four sides, or three sides with the empty throne as the fourth.

Note: no original rulebook survives. These are the usual modern reconstructions, close to the Copenhagen rules.

VI · 1732

Linnaeus in Lapland

By the 12th century chess was displacing tafl across Europe, and the exact rules were on the point of being lost. The game lingered in the far north. In 1732 the young Swedish naturalist Carl Linnaeus travelled through Lapland and noted how the Sámi played tablut, “Swedes against Muscovites”: sixteen Muscovites around a king and eight Swedes on a 9×9 board. He set it down in his Latin travel diary, Iter Lapponicum.

That note is one of the main sources for how a tafl game was really played. The other great source is a Welsh description of tawlbwrdd, written by Robert ap Ifan in 1587. Almost everything modern players use is stitched together from these two accounts and from the boards in the ground.

VII · Elder Futhark metrology

The Runes Counted

The Elder Futhark has twenty-four runes, in a fixed order, divided into three groups of eight called ættir: Freyr’s, Hagal’s and Tyr’s. Numbering each rune by its place in that order, from Fehu 1 to Othala 24, lets a word be added up letter by letter. The three ættir add up to 36, 100 and 164, and the whole futhark to 300. Two observations, offered as readings: HNEFA, the king, comes to 43, the same as TAFL, so the whole word hnefatafl is 43 + 43 = 86. And with Thurisaz as its first letter, TUFL comes to 27, which is 3×3×3, though that works for this one spelling only.

VIII · The Matrix of the North

A Reading

Everything on this page after the history is interpretation. This section gathers it into one reading, and is not established scholarship.

The board is the geography

The lopsided siege, a few defenders holding a king against a larger ring, echoes the hard facts of Northern warfare and of holding ground against a surrounding enemy.

The pieces are the culture

Glass, walrus ivory, bone and yew show how highly the game was valued. It sat at the centre of aristocratic life, and it went into the grave with the warrior.

The terminology is the math

The words of the game can be counted in the futhark: TAFL 43, HNEFI 50, HNEFA 43. In the framework of this site, hnefatafl is a kind of cognitive map of the Viking Age mind.

IX · The eternal siege

From a Roman soldiers’ game, to the Viking king’s table, to near-erasure by chess and a rescue by one traveller’s note in Lapland: Tafl survives today as a reconstructed game, and as one of the most carefully studied strategy games of the old world.
