History

History of Senet

Senet (written zenet in transliteration and often glossed as “passing”) is one of the oldest board games we know of. Boards and playing pieces appear in Egyptian burials from around 3000 BCE, and a Senet game is painted in the tomb of Hesy-Ra at Saqqara from about 2620 BCE. Mehen, the coiled snake board, is of similar age, so which game is “the oldest” is debated.

The game lasted for roughly three thousand years. Tutankhamun was buried with four Senet boxes, and a wall painting in the tomb of Queen Nefertari (about 1250 BCE) shows her playing it. Over time the game took on religious meaning: players pictured the pieces as the soul journeying through the underworld, and later boards give the last squares the names of divine places. A complete ancient set had five pieces per side, though some boards and modern reconstructions use more (up to ten). By the New Kingdom the pieces were often cones, spools or dog-headed figures. Egyptian funerary texts mention playing Senet in the afterlife: Chapter 17 of the Book of the Dead shows the dead person playing against an unseen opponent, and an earlier Coffin Text links the game to the passage between the living and the dead. Many Egyptologists think the game began as a pastime and gained its religious meaning over the centuries, but this is an inference from the evidence, not a recorded fact.

The ancient rules were never written down. What survives are the boards, the pieces, a few pictures and some hints in texts, so every modern version is a reconstruction. The best known come from R. C. Bell, Timothy Kendall and Peter Piccione, and they disagree on details. This page follows a Kendall-style version with a few simplifications of my own, listed below.

How to play

How to play

Setup

Thirty squares in three rows of ten. Read them as one winding path: row one left to right (1–10), row two right to left (11–20), row three left to right (21–30). Each side has five pieces, set on squares 1–10 alternating cyan, amber, cyan, amber. Cyan starts.

The throw

Four sticks, each with a light and a dark face. Count the light faces up: that is your move, from 1 to 4. If none are light you move 5. The chances are 1: 25%, 2: 37.5%, 3: 25%, 4: 6.25%, 5: 6.25%.

Moving

Move one piece forward by exactly the number thrown. You may not land on your own piece. If you have no legal move, your turn passes. A throw of 1, 4 or 5 earns another throw, a 2 or 3 does not.

Swapping

Landing on a single enemy piece swaps the two pieces. You cannot swap with a piece that has a friend directly beside it (a guarded pair), nor with any piece on a sanctuary square (15, 26, 28, 29, 30).

Walls

Three pieces in a row form a wall. An opponent cannot pass over it, so those pieces can hold a whole side back, until the wall has to move.

The last squares

Every piece must stop on square 26 first. From there it leaves with the exact throw (see the table). Squares 28, 29 and 30 need exactly 3, 2 and 1 to bear off, and a piece on 28 or 29 stays put until it gets that throw. First to bear off all five pieces wins.

Strategy

Strategy

Keep pairs

Two pieces side by side cannot be swapped. Moving one breaks the guard, so move pieces that are already alone first.

Build a wall

Three in a row stops everything behind it. It is strongest when the enemy has several pieces stuck there.

Plan for 26

Exact throws decide the end. A 2 is the commonest throw, so a piece two squares before 26 is well placed. The Water is one square past 26: a 1 from there costs you the piece’s whole journey.

Use the extra throws

1, 4 and 5 give another turn, and they are the throws that move you on or bear off. Keep options open so the extra throw is useful.

I · Historical Origins & Ritual Evolution

The Game of Passing

Ancient Egyptian zn.t: “passing”, in full zn.t n.t ḥꜥb, the passing game. It began as a game of skill and chance and became a map of the soul's journey through the Duat.

Senet is the oldest recorded grid-based board game, dating to Predynastic Egypt, c. 3100 BCE. First a secular strategy game on a 3 × 10 grid, it underwent a theological metamorphosis in the New Kingdom (c. 1550–1070 BCE).

By the Eighteenth Dynasty the board had become a cosmological simulator of the Duat. Its 5 to 7 pawns, the ibau or “dancers”, carried the Ba past archontic watchers, traps and halls of judgment. Complete sets were buried in royal tombs, most famously Tutankhamun's, alongside Chapter 17 of the Book of the Dead, as a ritual map of ascension.

Date: fragmentary boards that may be senet come from First Dynasty burials, c. 3100 BCE, which is Early Dynastic rather than Predynastic. The earliest secure depiction is in the tomb of Hesy-Re, c. 2620 BCE. It is “one of the oldest” rather than certainly the oldest: the 20-square game and Mehen are as old, and some scholars look for senet's origins in the Levant.

Religious turn: earlier than the New Kingdom. Old Kingdom tomb scenes already tie the game to the dead, and Coffin Text 405 makes it a link between the living and the dead. In the early 18th Dynasty it enters Book of the Dead 17, and in the 20th Dynasty the “Great Game Text” describes the board as the netherworld journey.

Pieces: a full set had 5 per player; the 7 come from Kendall's modern rules. I couldn't find a source for the name ibau, “dancers”, so please add yours. New Kingdom pieces were often cones and spools, or dog-headed.

Tutankhamun: his finest set (Cairo, JE 62058) is ebony and ivory on a sledge stand. A drawer held the pieces, knucklebones and throwsticks, and there's a 20-square game on the back. BD 17 is the text that names senet; it isn't a document buried “alongside” the set. “Archontic watchers” is your Gnostic lens on the Duat's gate-keepers.

IV · The Casting

Four sticks, five numbers

Senet was cast with flat throwsticks, one face light and one dark, or with knucklebones. In Kendall's reconstruction, count the light faces: 1, 2, 3 or 4. All dark counts as 5. A throw of 1, 4 or 5 earns another throw.

Cyan: the exact odds. Gold: your casts.

VII · One Continuous Lineage?

Senet · Tavli · Chess

Senet, Tavli and Chess form a single continuous lineage of ancient grid games. They were built as physical, interactive teaching tools for geometry, language and spiritual liberation.

Senet's 30 squares mirror Tavli's 30 checkers (15 waxing light against 15 waning dark) on a 24-point solar track.

On the historical record they are kin in spirit rather than one family tree. Tavli descends from the Roman tabula and ludus duodecim scriptorum and the Persian nard. Chess descends from Indian chaturanga, c. 600 CE. A direct descent from senet isn't documented, so present the lineage as a thematic one: race games of fate (Senet, Ur, Tavli) against war games of will (Chess).

Confirmed: Tavli does use 15 + 15 = 30 checkers on 24 points.
