The Jitterbug

Twelve points around a centre can fold and twist, with no edge ever changing length, from a cuboctahedron into an icosahedron and then an octahedron.

Buckminster Fuller called the twelve-point shape the Vector Equilibrium, and the motion the Jitterbug. On this page you can move it yourself, see its volume change, and read the checked arithmetic that sits beside it.

This first picture only swells and shrinks the twelve-point shape. It is not the twist. The real twist is next.

Why it works

Why no edge ever changes

Put the twelve points at (±a, ±b, 0), (0, ±a, ±b) and (±b, 0, ±a). Let the twist angle θ run from 0 to 60°, and let a and b move like this:

a(θ) = cosθ + sinθ / √3
b(θ) = cosθ − sinθ / √3
a² − ab + b² = cos²θ + sin²θ = 1
edge L = √(2 (a² − ab + b²)) = √2

Because a² − ab + b² is always exactly 1, the distance between neighbouring points is √2 at every angle. The shape can change completely while the strut lengths never change. That is what makes it a Jitterbug and not just a shape that stretches. At θ = 0 both a and b are 1 and we have the cuboctahedron; at 60° b is 0 and the points have met in pairs: the octahedron.

checkedThe paths of the twelve points are arcs of ellipses (switch on “Paths of the points” above). Checked by script at 0°, 22.24° and 60°.

What I noticed

A note from the body

Watching the shape close in toward its centre, I felt a tightness in the middle of my chest, like butterflies. When it opened outward again I felt released, as if it were stellating.Mandos, also known as ΦΥ ΨΗ

This is only what I noticed, not a measurement and not a claim. Try it for a minute or two and see what you notice.
