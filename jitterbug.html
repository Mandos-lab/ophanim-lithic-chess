The Jitterbug

Twelve points around a centre can fold and twist, with no edge ever changing length, from a cuboctahedron into an icosahedron and then an octahedron.

Buckminster Fuller called the twelve-point shape the Vector Equilibrium, and the motion the Jitterbug. On this page you can move it yourself, see its volume change, and read the arithmetic that sits beside it.

This first picture only swells and shrinks the twelve-point shape. It is not the twist. The real twist is next.

Why salt and the octahedron? Table salt crystallises as a cube, and breaks into smaller cubes at every size. Inside that cube each sodium sits at the centre of six chlorines, and those six points are an octahedron. Cube and octahedron are duals: each is built from the other’s centres.

01 · The exact mathematics

Why no edge ever changes

Think of a gold chain bracelet. Lay it flat, curl it round a wrist, fold it into a drawer: the shape changes completely, and not one link gets longer or shorter. The Jitterbug is that, in three dimensions. Twelve points, joined by struts that never change length, and a whole family of shapes living inside them.

The Vector Equilibrium is the cuboctahedron: the one arrangement in which the distance from the centre to every point equals the length of every edge (R = L = √2). Every spoke is as long as every rim vector, so the pulls in all directions cancel.

Under torque the 8 triangles keep their shape. They turn about their own axes while they move in toward the centre, and the 6 squares shear along a diagonal into pairs of triangles. Each of the twelve points travels along an ellipse:

u² + 3v² = 1   (the path of each point; semi-axes 1 and 1/√3 ≈ 0.577)
u = (a + b) / 2 = cosθ    v = (a − b) / 2 = sinθ / √3

Put the twelve points at (±a, ±b, 0), (0, ±a, ±b) and (±b, 0, ±a). Let the twist angle θ run from 0 to 60° (π/3), and let a and b move like this:

a(θ) = cosθ + sinθ / √3
b(θ) = cosθ − sinθ / √3
a² − ab + b² = cos²θ + sin²θ = 1
edge L = √(2 (a² − ab + b²)) = √2
volume (in Fuller’s unit) = 3 × the ordinary volume, summed over the faces: 20 → 18.51 → 4

Because a² − ab + b² is always exactly 1, the distance between neighbouring points is √2 at every angle. The shape can change completely while the strut lengths never change. That is what makes it a Jitterbug and not just a shape that stretches. At θ = 0 both a and b are 1 and we have the cuboctahedron; at 60° b is 0 and the points have met in pairs: the octahedron.

The paths of the twelve points are those ellipses. Switch on “Paths of the points” above to see them.

03 · The cell

The Jitterbug inside the cell

Everything on this page so far is rigid: struts that never change length. The cell is the opposite, soft and crowded and never still. Yet I keep finding the same pattern there: a fast trembling that takes you nowhere, then a sudden change of arrangement that does. That is what I mean by jitterbug in the living world, and I want to keep what has been measured apart from what is my own reading.

Measured means it comes from published measurements.   My reading means it is how I see it, and you are free to see it differently.

A bacterium swims by changing shape

Many bacteria, E. coli among them, swim with several helical flagella, each turned by its own rotary motor set in the cell wall. When the motors turn counter-clockwise (seen from the tip of a flagellum, looking back at the cell), the flagella wind together into one bundle and push the cell along in a straight run, typically about a second long.

When one or more motors reverse and turn clockwise, that flagellum leaves the bundle, the bundle comes apart, and the cell tumbles for a fraction of a second before setting off in a new direction. Run, tumble, run: a random walk. The cell steers by making its runs longer when things are getting better, which is chemotaxis, so a path that looks like trembling still drifts toward food.

Molecules never stop

Under the cell, molecules are in constant thermal motion. Bonds vibrate on timescales of tens of femtoseconds, which is why molecular-dynamics simulations advance in steps of about one or two femtoseconds. Small particles in water are knocked about by collisions, which is Brownian motion: any single path is jagged, while the crowd spreads out smoothly. The jitter is not noise laid on top of the physics. It is the physics.

Mitochondria rattle, then jump

Organelles such as mitochondria are not simply carried along tracks. The cytoplasm is itself stirred by motor proteins working on its filaments, so a mitochondrion rattles about where it is, which is called active diffusion, and now and then is taken up by kinesin or dynein and carried along a microtubule, while myosin pulls against actin. I am relying on the open-access paper Active diffusion and microtubule-based transport oppose myosin forces to position organelles in cells (PMC), which is about how these effects balance to place organelles inside a cell.

Mitochondria also show slow oscillations in their metabolism: membrane potential and reactive oxygen species can rise and fall in waves. That is a different thing from the rattling. I mention it only because the word oscillation covers both, and I do not want to blur them.

My reading My reading is geometric. A fast tremor with no net direction; a sudden reconfiguration that has one; and, underneath, something whose lengths stay fixed. In the Jitterbug the fixed thing is the strut. In the bacterium it is the helix of each flagellum, which keeps its shape while the bundle forms and breaks. In the mitochondrion I cannot yet point to a fixed thing. This is how I see it, not a finding.

04 · Sound and voice

Jitter and shimmer in the voice

Phoneticians already have a word for the trembling: jitter. When you hold a vowel, your vocal folds open and close about a hundred times a second, and no two cycles are quite alike. Jitter is the cycle-to-cycle variation in the length of the period. Shimmer is the same for loudness.

A healthy, steady voice has a little of both. In common voice-analysis software, local jitter above roughly one per cent and shimmer above roughly three to four per cent are flagged as unusual. A little makes a voice sound alive. A lot sounds rough or breathy. Underneath the trembling there is an average, and the ear calls that average the pitch.

05 · New technology

Where the pattern is being built

Engineers and chemists are now building things on these same ideas. Here is the short list I can stand behind. Most of it is research, not product.

  • Artificial bacterial flagella. Tiny magnetic helices turned by an external magnetic field so that they swim through fluid, studied for carrying drugs and for micro-surgery.
  • Active matter and microrobots. Swarms of self-propelled particles whose individual paths are jittery and whose group behaviour is organised.
  • DNA origami. DNA strands folded into designed shapes at the scale of nanometres (Paul Rothemund, 2006), including hollow polyhedra. The folding is the whole point.
  • Frames that change shape. A frame of fixed-length struts that reconfigures is the Jitterbug itself. The Hoberman sphere is a well-known relative: a different linkage, the same idea of a structure that grows and shrinks while no bar changes length.
