Quick rules

The king and his guard sit in the middle, and attackers twice as many ring the edges. Every piece moves like a rook. Sandwich an enemy piece in a straight line and it is removed. Defenders win if the king reaches a corner (on the 9×9 board, any edge square); attackers win if they capture the king. These are reconstructed rules: see “About these rules” below.

Game log

    History of Tafl

    Tafl (Old Norse tafl, “table” or “board game”) was the strategy game of the North for roughly eight hundred years, from about the 4th to the 12th century CE, long before chess reached Scandinavia. Its best-known form, hnefatafl, is named for the king piece, the hnefi, a word that also means “fist”. The game is lopsided on purpose: a king with a small guard must break out of a ring, and a force twice as large must hold the ring shut.

    It turns up all over the Viking world. In the Orkneyinga saga, Earl Rögnvald Kali puts tafl first in a verse about his nine skills (Tafl emk orr at efla, roughly “I am eager to play tafl”), with runes and verse-making on the same list. In the Hervarar saga, Odin, disguised as Gestumblindi, asks King Heidrek a riddle about the pieces: who are the maidens that fight weaponless around their lord, the dark ones always defending him and the fair ones attacking? In the Völuspá the gods play tafl in the meadow of Iðavöllr, and after Ragnarök they find their golden pieces again in the grass.

    Boards and pieces survive. A board from the Gokstad ship burial in Norway (around 900 CE) has a tafl grid on one side and a Nine Men’s Morris board on the other. The Ballinderry board from Ireland (10th century) is a small yew board of 7×7 squares with a peg hole in each. Graves at Birka in Sweden hold sets of carved gaming pieces, and a blue-and-white glass piece found at Lindisfarne (8th to 9th century) was announced in 2020.

    After about 1100 chess slowly pushed tafl aside, but the game lived on in the far north. In 1732 the Swedish naturalist Carl Linnaeus, travelling through Lapland, wrote down how the Sámi played tablut, a 9×9 relative with sixteen attackers (his “Muscovites”) against eight defenders and a king (his “Swedes”). A good part of what we know about how tafl was really played comes from his note and from a Welsh description of tawlbwrdd written in 1587.

    How to play

    The sides

    Attackers (amber) start around the edges, defenders (cyan) and the king (white, gold ring) in the middle. Attackers outnumber defenders about two to one: 24 against 12 and a king on 11×11, 16 against 8 on 9×9, 8 against 4 on 7×7.

    Moving

    Attackers move first. Every piece, the king included, slides any distance along a row or column over empty squares, never diagonally and never jumping. Only the king may stop on the throne in the centre or on a corner. Other pieces may pass over the empty throne.

    Capturing

    When your move leaves an enemy piece in a straight line between the piece you moved and another of your pieces, it is removed. The empty throne, and the corners where they are refuges, count as enemies to everyone. The king helps to capture. You may step safely between two enemy pieces.

    The king

    On 11×11 the king is captured only when attackers hold all four sides, or three sides with the empty throne as the fourth. On the smaller boards he is weaker: two attackers in a line take him, unless he stands on or beside the throne. He can never be captured against the edge.

    Winning

    Defenders win when the king reaches a corner, or on 9×9 any edge square. Attackers win by capturing the king. A side with no legal move loses. The same position three times, or 400 moves, is a draw.

    Strategy

    Defenders: open two roads

    A king with one open road is stopped by one blocker. A king with two open roads wins, because a single move cannot block both. Look for squares where the king threatens two corners (or two edges) at once.

    Defenders: thin the ring

    Use your own pieces to capture attackers that wander from their friends. Every capture opens a gap, and the king only needs one.

    Attackers: seal the corners

    On the boards with corner refuges, attackers placed two squares along each edge from a corner and one on the diagonal make that corner very hard to enter. Do this before you chase the king.

    Attackers: stay in pairs

    A lone attacker on an open line is easy to sandwich, and one next to a corner can be captured against it. Keep your pieces linked, and close lines rather than chasing the king.

    Both: count the roads

    Before every move, count the lines the king could use next turn. The status bar warns you when the king threatens to escape.

    Count a word in Elder Futhark

    Positions run from Fehu 1 to Othala 24. c counts as k, v as w, y as i; letters the futhark lacks are skipped.

    The three ættir (the groups of eight) add up to 36, 100 and 164, and the whole futhark to 300. Two observations, offered as readings: HNEFA, the king, comes to 43, the same as TAFL, so the whole word hnefatafl is 43 + 43 = 86; and with Thurisaz as its first letter TUFL comes to 27, which is 3×3×3, though that works for this one spelling only.

    Notes. The order of the 24 runes is attested: the Kylver stone from Gotland (about 400 CE) carries the whole futhark in this order. The numbers 1 to 24 are simply positions in that order. No ancient source gives the runes numeric values, so everything on this page that adds them up is a reading in the Phonetic Archaeology manner, not scholarship. Elder Futhark was in use from about the 2nd to the 8th century; the Viking-age words tafl, hnefi and konungr were written in the 16-rune Younger Futhark, so spelling them in Elder Futhark is a modern exercise.

    About these rules

    Nobody has an original rulebook for Tafl, so every modern game is a reconstruction. This page uses the common modern reading, close to the Copenhagen rules: rook moves, capture by sandwiching, a restricted throne, hostile empty throne and corners, an armed king, and Attackers moving first. Hnefatafl 11×11 uses corner escape and a king taken on four sides. Brandubh 7×7 uses corner escape and a weaker king. Tablut 9×9 uses edge escape and a weaker king, the usual reading of Linnaeus’s notes. Left out for simplicity: shieldwall captures, edge forts, and the win by surrounding every defender. The computer opponent looks two or three moves ahead; it is a fair opponent, not a champion.
