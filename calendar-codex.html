I · The year drawn as a circle

Why 360

For thousands of years skywatchers laid the year out as a circle of 360 parts: twelve months of thirty days. The Babylonian star calendar MUL.APIN uses a 12 × 30 “schematic year”, and the civil calendar of Egypt was built on twelve months of thirty days. It was a tidy ideal, not a measurement of the sky.

The number 360 is rich in divisors. It has 24 of them, so halves, thirds, quarters, fifths, sixths, eighths, ninths, tenths and twelfths all come out whole. That is also why a circle has 360 degrees.

It is also why “one degree a day” is such an appealing picture of the Sun’s path. The picture is slightly false. Over a real year the Sun moves 360° in 365.2422 days, which is about 0.9856° a day.

II · The year the sky keeps

365.2422 Days

The tropical year, from one spring equinox to the next, lasts about 365.2422 days: 365 days, 5 hours, 48 minutes and 45 seconds. A calendar year that is a different length slides against the seasons. Choose a calendar and a span of years and see how far.

DRIFT AGAINST THE SEASONS

Torque and friction

The deck pictures the gap as a perfect 360-day circle set against the real year: ideal baseline 360.0000 days, tropical solar year 365.2422 days, annual variance +5.2422 days, drawn as torque and friction. Its analysis: “Physical planetary mechanics do not obey perfect sexagesimal geometry. The Earth’s wobble and elliptical orbit generate an ongoing, compounding friction against the 360° baseline. A rigid 360-day calendar without intercalary adjustment would drift: solstices and equinoxes slide backwards through the months.”

Check

The drift is real, and its cause is plainer than friction: the year is simply 5.2422 days longer than 360. The ellipse of the orbit makes the four seasons unequal, from about 89 to about 94 days. The wobble, the slow turning of the Earth’s axis once in about 25,770 years, is why the tropical year (365.2422) is about 20 minutes shorter than the sidereal year (365.2564).

III · Five days outside the count

The Epagomenal Days

Egypt kept the thirty-day months and added five days at the end of the year, giving 12 × 30 + 5 = 365. These are the epagomenal days, from the Greek hēmerai epagomenai, “days added on”. In the myth, told by Plutarch, they are the birthdays of Osiris, Isis, Horus the Elder, Set and Nephthys, days that belonged to no month.

The Maya did the same thing with their own numbers. The 360-day tun sits inside the 365-day Haab’: eighteen months of twenty days, then five days called Wayeb’.

Five days close most of the gap but not all of it. 365 is still 0.2422 of a day short, so the Egyptian civil year slid one day against the seasons about every four years, and needed roughly 1,460 years to travel all the way round: the Sothic cycle. Julius Caesar’s reform, from 45 BCE, added a leap day every fourth year (365.25). The Gregorian reform of 1582 dropped three leap days in every 400 years (365.2425) and skipped ten calendar days to catch up.

The deck draws the five days as a valve between two 360-day wheels: the Maya tun venting through Wayeb’, and the Egyptian civil calendar venting through the birthdays of Osiris, Isis, Horus, Set and Nephthys. Its key insight: ancient metrologists did not destroy the 360° baseline. They kept the 360-day core to preserve sexagesimal and divinatory harmony. The five extra days lived outside the geometric law, absorbing the +5.2422-day torque without corrupting the pristine 360° mathematics.

IV · The 13 × 28 claim

Thirteen Moons

The claim goes like this: thirteen months of 28 days (364 days, plus one) is the natural Moon calendar, and it was taken from us. Three things in it can be checked.

The Moon is not 28 days

The Moon’s phases repeat every 29.53 days (the synodic month), and it circles the sky once in 27.32 days (the sidereal month). Thirteen real lunar months last 13 × 29.53 = 383.9 days, nearly 19 days longer than the solar year.

A year holds 12.37 moons

365.2422 ÷ 29.53 is about 12.37, so a lunar calendar needs a thirteenth month only now and then: seven times in 19 years. Real lunisolar calendars use 12 months and an occasional leap month.

It is a modern design

Thirteen months of 28 days were proposed by Auguste Comte in his Positivist calendar (1849) and by Moses Cotsworth as the International Fixed Calendar (1902), which Kodak used internally for decades. Both are solar and neither claims ancient origin. The 1582 reform removed ten days, not a month.

To be fair to it: every month starts on the same weekday, 364 divides into 52 exact weeks and four quarters of 91 days, and with a Year Day and a Leap Day it follows the Gregorian year. Its weakness is the story told about it, not its arithmetic. One more check, from the 360 side: 360 ÷ 13 is 27.692… degrees, so thirteen equal months cannot tile the circle in whole degrees, which is what the 24 divisors of 360 are good at.

13 × 28 = 364, plus a day

The deck’s formula: 13 months × 28 days = 364 days, with one day out of time. Its charge: marketed as a natural and harmonic alternative to the Gregorian system, the calendar borrows the look of ancient lore while ignoring orbital mechanics, and forces a symmetry (13 × 28) that matches neither the geometric ideal of 360 nor the true lunar and solar cycles.

Check. The mismatch with the Moon and with 360 is right. As a solar-count design it does work, and says so openly.

Sidereal against synodic

The sidereal month, 27.32 days, brings the Moon back to the same background stars. The synodic month, 29.53 days, brings it back to the same phase. The deck’s overshoot: 13 × 29.53 = 383.89 days, nearly 19 days past the solar year, so a 13 × 28 calendar is cut off from the lunar phases it claims to honour.

Check. 13 × 29.53059 = 383.9 days and 383.9 − 365.2422 = 18.66. Both hold.

V · How the Moon’s calendars really work

Twelve Months and a Leap

Twelve synodic months last 354.37 days, about eleven days short of the solar year. The Babylonian, Greek and Hebrew calendars kept the Moon’s phases and the seasons together by adding a leap month. The pattern that works best is the Metonic cycle: 19 solar years hold almost exactly 235 lunar months (6,939.60 days against 6,939.69), which means seven leap months in 19 years.

The deck puts it this way: to keep the 354-day lunar year locked to the 365-day solar seasons, seven intercalary leap months are added over a 19-year cycle, and the Babylonians, Greeks and Hebrews saw that 19 tropical solar years equal 235 synodic lunar months.

The purely lunar calendar of Islam makes the opposite choice. It never adds a month, and so its months wander through every season over about 33 years.

VII · A reading

The Handshake

Everything above is astronomy and history. This section is interpretation, in the framework of Mandos16, and is not established science.

360 is the fixed law

The circle of 360 is the unchanging baseline: whole numbers, many divisors, no remainder. In the framework this is the objective baseline, O(t).

The sky adds a remainder

The real year is 5.2422 days longer. Five days can be added as a whole number, and 0.2422 is left over: no whole number of days will ever hold it, so calendars keep adding leap days and leap months. The flaw, in this reading, is what keeps the system working.
