The Arabic Abjad
28 Letters, 28 Mansions

Twenty-eight letters carry the values 1 to 1000. This page reads the Arabic abjad four ways, sets the fourteen luminous letters beside their sum, plots the letters on a 36-node wheel, and checks each claim against what the sources say.

I · The Linguistic Architecture and the Textual History

Roots, patterns and a road of inscriptions

Arabic is built on roots and patterns. Three consonants carry the meaning and the vowels and extra letters carry the pattern. The root K-T-B, “write”, gives kataba (he wrote), kitāb (book), kutub (books), maktab (office, desk) and kātib (writer). The letters are the skeleton of the word, which is why a letter-value reading has a natural home here. The throat carries the deepest sounds: the pharyngeals ḥ (ح) and ʿ (ع), the uvulars q (ق), kh (خ) and gh (غ), and the four emphatics ṭ ṣ ḍ ẓ (ط ص ض ظ).

The script has a long road behind it:

  • 853 BCE. The Kurkh Monolith of the Assyrian king Shalmaneser III names an Arab chief, Gindibu, who brought a thousand camels to the battle of Qarqar. It is the first written mention of Arabs.
  • About 1st century BCE to 4th century CE. Safaitic, Thamudic and the other desert inscriptions of the north carve Old North Arabian in their own alphabets. They are cousins of the Arabic script, not its parents.
  • 1st to 4th century CE. The Arabic script grows out of the Nabataean Aramaic cursive. The En Avdat inscription in the Negev already mixes Nabataean letters with Arabic words. The Namara inscription (328 CE), the epitaph of Imruʾ al-Qays, “king of all the Arabs”, is written in Nabataean letters in a language close to Arabic.
  • 7th century CE. The Quran is written down and copied as a standard text. Dots (iʿjām) are added to tell apart letters that share one shape (ب ت ث, ج ح خ), and marks for the short vowels (ḥarakāt) follow.
  • Two orders. The old order ʾ-B-J-D (أبجد) is the Phoenician and Hebrew sequence. It serves numbers, chronograms (dates written as a word whose letters add to the year) and the numerals of astronomical tables. The modern dictionary order ʾ-B-T-Th (ا ب ت ث) groups letters by shape and serves lists, dictionaries and schools.

Check. The root-and-pattern forms are the standard textbook set (CaCaCa, CiCāC, CuCuC, maCCaC, CāCiC). The contrast with Indo-European is one of degree, not a wall: English has sing, sang, song. The abjad has 28 letters. Some counts add the hamza (ء) as a 29th.

Dates and names, from the standard references and not opened while building this page, so check them before relying on them: the Kurkh Monolith and Gindibu (853 BCE, the battle of Qarqar); Namara dated 328 CE; En Avdat, usually placed in the 1st to 2nd century CE with the exact date debated. Safaitic, Thamudic, Dadanitic and Hismaic belong to the Ancient North Arabian family, which is related to the Arabic script but is not where it came from.

The dots and vowel marks are credited by tradition to scholars of the late 7th and 8th centuries: Naṣr ibn ʿĀṣim and Yaḥyā ibn Yaʿmar for the dots, Abū al-Aswad al-Duʾalī for the first vowel points and al-Khalīl ibn Aḥmad for the shapes used today. Some 7th-century papyri already carry dots, so the change was gradual and older than the story. The shape-based dictionary order is also credited to Naṣr ibn ʿĀṣim.

In astronomy the abjad numerals were used for degrees and minutes in sexagesimal tables. That use is real and old. The bearings on this page (value mod 360) are our own device.

II · The 28-Letter Abjad Master Table

Twenty-eight letters, values 1 to 1000

The archaic order ʾ-B-J-D spans the units (1 to 9), the tens (10 to 90), the hundreds (100 to 900) and ends on ghayn, 1000. Check. Every value, bearing and node was recomputed by program. The 28 values add to 5995. That is the 27-value set that also adds to 4995 in Greek, plus ghayn’s 1000. Ten letters fall on shared nodes: 4 (dāl, mīm, tāʾ), 8 (ḥāʾ, fāʾ, ḍād), 20 (kāf, rāʾ), 24 (sīn, khāʾ) and 28 (qāf, ghayn). Only ẓāʾ (900) falls on node 0, and only ʿayn (70) falls on node 34, the same node as the Greek ΝΟΜΟΣ (430).

These are the eastern (Mashriqi) values. The western (Maghribi) tradition gives different values to several letters, including س ش ص ض ظ غ, so any sum with those letters changes there. The element sequence is traditional and is credited to al-Būnī. I could not check that attribution. The jīm is /d͡ʒ/ in the table, but is pronounced differently across the Arabic-speaking world (for example /g/ in Egypt, /ʒ/ in the Levant).

III · The Four Depths (Marātib al-Ḥisāb)

Great, spreading, small, smallest

There are four depths of reckoning. Kabīr (the great) is the plain sum. Bast (the spreading) spells out every letter’s name and adds those letters too. Ṣaghīr (the small) reduces each letter to its first digit, 1 to 9. Aṣghar (the smallest) reduces the Kabīr total to a single digit by adding its digits. Type any Arabic word.

DepthResultHow

Check. Kabīr, ṣaghīr and the digit-adding step are well defined. The names and the exact method differ between authors, so treat the four-name scheme as this page’s working set. For the Aṣghar step here is a fact that holds by construction: each letter’s first digit leaves the same remainder mod 9 as its value does (20 → 2, 300 → 3, 1000 → 1). So the digital root of the Ṣaghīr sum is always the same as the Aṣghar. The package listed the ṣaghīr as a digital root, which would make it the same as the Aṣghar, so here the ṣaghīr keeps its own sum.

The Bast depends on how each letter name is spelled. Alif is ألف (111), lām is لام (71), hāʾ is spelled ها (6), which gives Allāh = 111 + 71 + 71 + 6 = 259. Spell hāʾ as هاء and the same word gives 260. The names used here: ألف باء جيم دال ها واو زاي حاء طاء ياء كاف لام ميم نون سين عين فاء صاد قاف راء شين تاء ثاء خاء ذال ضاد ظاء غين. Check the spelling before comparing with a source.

Hamza, tāʾ marbūṭa, alif maqṣūra and the other spelling variants are folded to their plain letters (ء to alif, ة to ه, ى to ي, آ أ إ to alif). Marks for short vowels, shadda and the dagger alif are ignored. Allāh has two lām written, so it adds to 66.

IV · The Luminous Letters (Al-Muqaṭṭaʿāt)

Fourteen letters and the sum 693

Fourteen of the 28 letters open Quranic chapters as the muqaṭṭaʿāt, the “cut-off letters”, in 29 chapters. In the tradition they are the luminous letters (al-ḥurūf al-nūrāniyya): ا 1 ه 5 ح 8 ط 9 ي 10 ك 20 ل 30 م 40 ن 50 س 60 ع 70 ص 90 ق 100 ر 200

Together they add to 693 = 3² × 7 × 11, and 693 + 1 = 694. A mnemonic holds all fourteen: نَصٌّ حَكِيمٌ قَاطِعٌ لَهُ سِرٌّ, “a wise, decisive text with a secret”. The commonest openers:

In Sufi readings, الم runs from the throat (alif) through the tongue (lām) to the lips (mīm), which is the path of the voice from deepest to outermost.

Check. The 14 letters, 693 = 3² × 7 × 11, 71, 231, 70 and 195 were recomputed. The other 14 letters add to 5302, so the 28 add to 5995. The mnemonic does contain exactly the 14 letters.

The +1 (694) is the Hebrew kolel habit of adding one for the whole word. It is not an Arabic practice, so it is the Codex’s own step. The 231 of الر matches the 231 gates of the Sefer Yetzirah, which count the pairs of 22 Hebrew letters (22 × 21 ÷ 2). The two numbers agree, but they come from different counts, so this is a coincidence of arithmetic and not a shared structure.

The luminous-and-dark split and the throat, tongue and lips reading are traditional, and I attribute the second to Ibn ʿArabī from memory only. The sound order is correct: alif is glottal, lām is made with the tongue and mīm with the lips.

V · The 36-Node Wheel

Twenty-eight letters on thirty-six nodes

Each letter sits on the node of its value mod 36. Letters that share a node stack inward. The gold ring marks the 14 luminous letters. Tap a letter for its details. Type a word in Section III and its letters join in order, with a dashed ring for the node of its total.

Check. The positions are the node column of the master table. Five nodes are shared (4, 8, 20, 24, 28), so some letters sit inside others. Node 0 holds ẓāʾ alone, which the Codex calls the origin. ʿAyn (70) falls on node 34 and no other letter does.

The four colours follow the cycle Fire, Air, Water, Earth in letter order. They are not four quarters of the circle, since the letters are placed by value and not by order. The traditional link of the 28 letters to the 28 lunar mansions (manāzil al-qamar) is attributed to al-Būnī, and I did not check it.

Allāh = 66 = 11 × 6, and Huwa = 11. The Basmala has 19 letters, the same as Wāḥid (the One) = 19. The Bast of Allāh, the names of its four letters, is 111 + 71 + 71 + 6 = 259, which is Ibrāhīm. The Basmala adds to 786, and 786 mod 360 = 66, the value of Allāh.

The Saturnian square of Allāh (a 3×3 square, the square of Saturn) has centre 22 and base (66 − 12) ÷ 3 = 18. Its rows, columns and diagonals each add to 66, and each number is turned into letters (taḥwīl):

Check. Every sum was recomputed. The Basmala’s 786 uses ar-Raḥmān without the dagger alif (329, not 330). Here is what lies behind the 66: the Basmala is 66 (Allāh) plus 102 + 329 + 289, and those three add to exactly 720 = 2 × 360. So 786 mod 360 = 66 is no accident of the mod, since Allāh sits inside the Basmala. The real fact is that the other three words add to two full turns. The eastern values are used throughout, and the Basmala has س, so the western values would give another sum.

Parities with Greek, both checked: Nūḥ = 64 = 4³ = 8² matches Ἀλήθεια (α1 + λ30 + η8 + θ9 + ε5 + ι10 + α1 = 64), and Ibrāhīm = 259 = 7 × 37 matches Βασιλεία = 259, the word on the Lord’s Prayer page. Adam = 45 = 1 + 2 + … + 9. The “eight survivors” of the ark come from Genesis, since the Quran does not give a number.

A caution on all of these: with many words to try, some equalities will turn up by chance, and the page does not claim more than the sums.
