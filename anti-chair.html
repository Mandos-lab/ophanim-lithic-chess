The Anti-Chair and the Resonant Monolith

When does a thing become an object? And what happens when you try to turn money into stone?

Start with an old armchair in a patch of morning sun. Then look at Bitcoin. They answer the same question in opposite ways, and the difference tells you who is carrying the weight.

01 · The problem of objectivity

A dispatch that fit the seam

I had spent a day and a quiet morning at my thesis on the armchair in the sunny corner of my study, testing it against Martin Heidegger, Immanuel Kant, Donald Hoffman’s interface theory and the physics of impedance. I had reached one conclusion: a chair becomes an object only when a three-way interface locks, and it quietens the body so well that the mammal can afford to contemplate.

I put my tools down and opened my timeline. At the top of the feed was a note from Matt:

“The best argument against Bitcoin isn’t that it’s imaginary. It’s that volatility, custody risk and adoption uncertainty are real. At least bring your best objections to the fight.”
— Matt, @MatthewRyanCase

It felt like a catch on a fishing line: you chew on a seam in the world and the world drops the right mirror in front of you. I have held Bitcoin since 2016, apart from one three-month detour into other tokens before I came back to the bedrock. Matt was saying that Bitcoin’s weak points are not imaginary but real. Fair. But my work on impedance had just shown me what “real” means in the physics of things.

Philosophers have long asked whether objectivity lives in some hidden essence, the Kantian thing-in-itself. In acoustics and electronics there is no hidden ghost in the wood. A thing counts as an object when the meeting between two systems settles into balance without a nasty distortion. The balance has two parts: R, the resistance, the real pushback of matter that turns a knock into a little heat; and X, the reactance, the lag between how slowly matter changes and how fast a nervous system refreshes.

Z = R + jX

02 · The chair in the sun

Four tests an object must pass

When does the wooden armchair in its dusty shaft of light become an object? Not by accident, and not by decree. It takes four things at once.

I

Stillness

An armchair falling from a plane, crushed in a press or on fire gives you nothing to contemplate. It only calls up fight-or-flight. Looking at timber as shape is a luxury that only a settled thing can give.

II

Balance

Mortise-and-tenon joints give plenty of real resistance R. The cellulose and lignin give a long phase lag X: a century of slow wood against a millisecond of nerve. Together they make Z = R + jX.

III

No ghost

There is no hidden “chairness” in the joints. What a measurement misses is only grain for geometry and fading light for the sitter. The remainder is the limit of the sieve, not an essence.

IV

The subsidy

The chair takes the weight, cradles the spine and pays the glucose bill. My stabilising muscles stop burning energy to hold me up, and that spare bandwidth is free to enter νόησις (noesis), the act of thinking, and meet τό Νοούμενον, the thing thought.

That fourth point is the one I care about most: matter subsidises spirit. A good object is one that carries the load so you can look away from it and think.

03 · The resonant monolith

Bitcoin, the anti-chair

Now leave the armchair and look at the large cryptographic machine called Bitcoin. Matt was right that volatility, custody risk and slow adoption are real. Where I differ is on why. They are not small bugs that a fund or a friendly app will iron out. They are the shape of its objectivity: an interface working very hard to pretend it is a stone.

1

A ceiling that cannot bend

Timber gives a little under weight. Bitcoin’s supply cannot: 21,000,000 and no more. Since supply cannot flex, every shock to the economy, liquidity or mood goes straight into the price.

2

Rules that set hard

Changing a core rule takes nearly everyone agreeing, and when they do not agree the chain can split, as it did in the block-size fight that gave us Bitcoin Cash. The protocol keeps its shape because it will not flex.

3

Custody strain

Holding your own coins leaves no room for human frailty. Lose or mistype one 24-word seed phrase and the loss is final. Many people end up handing their coins back to a custodian, which is the very thing the stone was meant to avoid.

Here is where the two part ways. A chair damps: it takes the knock and turns it into a little warmth (R is large). Bitcoin has almost no damping (R close to zero), so it behaves like a high-Q resonator, a bell. Every tremor in the money world rings through it, and the ringing arrives as price swings and human worry. See the lab below.

There is a second difference, and it is about rest. You can fall asleep in a chair and forget it exists. A coin you hold alone asks for a watchful eye: if you stop paying attention, a key can be lost, or the value can halve before you look up.

The chair takes the load so the human can rest. Bitcoin asks the human to take the shock so the code can stay pure.

This is a philosophical essay, not financial advice. It says nothing about whether you should buy, hold or sell.

08 · The verdict

Who carries the strain

The chair in the sunny corner is an object because it settles your weight, damps the world’s noise, and lets you forget it is there.
Bitcoin is the anti-chair: a machine so afraid of bending to human frailty that it asks you to stand at attention forever, holding up the very stone that was meant to hold you.
We should judge our structures not by the purity of their code or the strictness of their edges, but by who, or what, is asked to take the strain.

Money that tries to become stone has to ask: stone for whom? A good object gives rest to the one who lives with it. That is a test worth carrying to every tool you are handed, from a chair to a coin to a rule.
